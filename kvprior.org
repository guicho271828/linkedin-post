Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is now standard: fetch documents, put them in the prompt, answer.
Its major bottleneck is prefill, i.e., computing the KV cache of all the retrieved documents
before the first token comes out.

But the same documents are retrieved again and again across many user queries.
So why not prefill each document once, offline, store its cache, and just concatenate them later?

Because transformer KV caches are prefix-dependent.
A cache computed for "doc A at the start of the prompt" is not the same as "doc A after docs B and C".
Naively concatenating them (no recompute) causes substantial quality degradation.

[figure explaining above]

Existing fixes recover the quality by recomputing some tokens at inference time,
fine-tuning the model to produce composable caches, or learning "link tokens" between documents.
They work, but they cost runtime or training, and the mechanism remains poorly understood.
(What are the link tokens actually learning?)

Conventional wisdom says the degradation comes from two sources:
(A) the lost cross-attention between documents, and
(B) position-specific artifacts baked into the cache.
Many methods restore (A), which requires online computation.
To our knowledge, nobody tried to directly remove (B).

So we looked at the attention logits.
Individually prefilled caches show huge "attention sink" spikes at the beginning of every document.
A RoPE reset does not remove them, because models learn their own implicit positional encoding,
and the BOS token leaves a boundary effect that propagates over layers.
Even KVPacket's learned embeddings do not remove the spikes; they just absorb them.

Meanwhile, in a regular full prefill, documents that are *not* at the beginning have few spikes.
That suggests a trivially simple fix:

1. Prepend a random, irrelevant prefix (sampled from the training set) to the document.
2. Prefill, discard the prefix part, and re-rotate RoPE.
3. Repeat with ~4-8 different prefixes and average the caches.

That's it. We call it KVPrior. Offline, training-free, zero inference overhead.
Averaging leaves only what is common across contexts, i.e., the document itself.
The attention spikes are gone, closely resembling full prefill.

Why "Prior"? Because it is a Bayesian story.
We assume, as a prior, that retrieved documents contribute independently (a mean-field assumption).
One may think individual prefill already computes p(doc), but it does not:
it computes p(doc | BOS or empty prefix), which is a biased sample.
KVPrior instead estimates the marginal over arbitrary prefixes of arbitrary lengths.
When the prior is wrong (e.g., multi-hop reasoning),
cache recomputation becomes the posterior update.

Averaging caches is not the same as averaging output distributions, though.
A Taylor expansion shows the gap is governed by the Hessian along the direction of prefix noise.
We measured it: the total variation is comparable to that of FP8/INT8 quantization.

Results on Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-4B and Granite-4.1-8B over 5 RAG benchmarks:
- Biography / Needle-in-a-Haystack (independent documents): KVPrior nearly recovers full prefill,
  and achieves the best accuracy-runtime Pareto front on Biography.
- Multi-hop QA (HotpotQA, Musique, 2WikiQA): modest gains alone, on par with KVPacket
  (trained on the entire dataset, vs. our 4-8 sampled prefixes),
  but KVPrior + recomputation beats recomputation alone.
- Out-of-distribution: prefixes sampled from Biography still help on other datasets,
  while KVLink trained on Biography struggles to generalize.

As few as 4 random prefixes are enough. Sometimes the boring idea wins.

This also matters beyond RAG: LLM pipelines and "LLMs inside algorithms"
constantly reuse the same strings at different positions across calls.

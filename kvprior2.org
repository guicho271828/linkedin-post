Why does "stitching together" cached documents hurt RAG accuracy -- and can we fix it for free?

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is everywhere now, but it is expensive:
every time a user query comes in, the LLM has to re-process (prefill) the
retrieved documents before it can even start generating an answer. Since the
same documents get retrieved again and again across different queries, the
obvious fix is to precompute and cache their KV representations once, then just
reuse them.

The problem: naively concatenating individually-prefilled KV caches harms
accuracy because transformer KV caches are prefix-dependent.
A cache computed for "doc A at the start of the prompt" is not the same as "doc A after docs B and C".
Naively concatenating them causes substantial quality degradation.

This problem also matters beyond RAG: LLM pipelines and harnesses
that implement "LLMs inside algorithms", like Mellea (mellea.ai), LangChain, or SGLang,
constantly reuse the same strings at different positions across calls.
We already covered their surprising efficiency of such harnesses in the Verbalized Algorithm (VAs) post,
but runtime complexity of VAs are still affected by this prefix-only cache assumptions.

The usual explanation for the degradation is "attention sinks" and lost cross-document
attention, and the usual fixes are expensive -- recomputing parts of the cache
online, fine-tuning the model, or training extra "link" tokens to glue documents
together and recover cross-attention.
All of these cost either latency or GPU-hours, and none of them really
explain *why* they work.

In our new paper, "KVPrior: On the Surprising Effectiveness of Marginalizing
KVCaches over Prefixes" (MIT-IBM Watson AI Lab), we dug into this and found
something simpler going on: each individually-prefilled cache is implicitly
conditioned on *one specific prefix* -- usually the empty one, or just a BOS
token. That's a biased, not a representative, snapshot of the document.

[figure explaining kvprior]

So instead of training anything, we just... average the bias out. KVPrior prefills
each document behind a handful of random, irrelevant prefixes, discards the
prefix tokens, reset RoPE, and averages the resulting caches. Under the
assumption that retrieved documents are roughly independent, this estimates the
*prefix-marginal* cache -- the representation the document "should" have had
regardless of what happened to precede it.
*That's it. We call it KVPrior.* Training-free, zero inference overhead,
and it works with as few as 4-8 sampled prefixes.
Averaging leaves only what is common across random contexts, i.e., the document itself.
The attention sinks are gone, closely resembling full prefill.

[figure showing attention sinks]

Why "Prior"? Because it is a Bayesian story.
We assume, as a prior, that retrieved documents contribute independently (a mean-field assumption).
One may think individual prefill already computes p(doc), but it does not:
it computes p(doc | BOS or empty prefix), which is a biased sample.
KVPrior instead estimates the marginal over arbitrary prefixes of arbitrary lengths.
When the prior is wrong (e.g., multi-hop reasoning),
cache recomputation becomes the posterior update.

The RAG performance surprised us too:

- On tasks where documents genuinely are independent (Biography,
  Needle-in-a-Haystack), KVPrior nearly recovers full-prefill accuracy and beats
  existing trained/recomputation-based methods.

- On multi-hop reasoning benchmarks (HotpotQA, Musique, 2WikiQA), where
  documents really do depend on each other, the gains are more modest -- but
  KVPrior stacks cleanly on top of existing recomputation methods, pushing them
  to new state-of-the-art results.

- It also generalizes to out-of-distribution prefixes far better than trained
  alternatives like KVLink, which degrade sharply once you leave the
  training distribution.

Averaging caches is not the same as averaging output distributions, though.
A Taylor expansion shows the averaging
works precisely when the Hessian of the model's next-token function is small in
the direction of prefix variation -- i.e., when the prefix really is just noise
relative to the document's own content.
We verify this empirically by measuring
how close KVPrior's output distribution is to the true prefix-marginal
distribution -- the gap is comparable to the noise introduced by ordinary
FP8/INT8 quantization.

[table showing the gap]

Sometimes the simplest idea -- "just average over the thing you want to be
invariant to" -- beats a learned fix, and even tells you something about why the
learned fix worked in the first place.
